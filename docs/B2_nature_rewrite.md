# B.2 model and optimisation rewrite

## B.2.1 AIE-calibrated reduced reaction–transport model

We developed an aggregation-induced emission (AIE)-calibrated, reduced reaction–transport model to predict the spatial degree of cure during a single projected exposure. The model resolves four processes in their physical order: formation of the local light field, transport and depletion of oxygen, transport and depletion of 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidin-1-oxyl (TEMPO), and conversion after the local inhibitor reservoir has been exhausted. For an input grayscale mask and nominal irradiance, the incident field is calculated as follows:

**Local irradiance = nominal irradiance × (optical point-spread function convolved with the grayscale mask).**

where \(G_{\sigma_{\mathrm{opt}}}\) is the measured or fitted optical point-spread function. This operation represents the finite projector focus, scattering and pixel cross-talk that redistribute light before reaction. It therefore converts the commanded mask into the irradiance field that drives every subsequent state update. Similar convolutional light-field descriptions have been used to predict and compensate for optical spread in projection photopolymerisation.<sup>4,6,11,20,21</sup>

The chemical state is represented by two energy-equivalent inhibitor-capacity fields, \(H_{\mathrm{O_2}}(\mathbf{x},t)\) and \(H_{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{x},t)\), and a productive post-inhibition dose, \(D_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathbf{x},t)\). At each interval \(\Delta t\), oxygen and TEMPO are first transported using Gaussian propagators with standard deviations \(\sqrt{2D_j\Delta t}/\Delta x\). The propagators approximate the discrete Green-function solution of Fickian diffusion for constant diffusivity \(D_j\). The available exposure increment, \(I\Delta t\), then depletes oxygen. Any remainder depletes TEMPO, and only the exposure remaining after both capacities reach zero contributes to \(D_{\mathrm{p}}\). The sequential update is an operator-split approximation to the stronger early competition of oxygen and radical scavengers with propagation; it is not intended to represent every elementary collision. Detailed reaction–diffusion models establish the importance of oxygen transport and radical trapping, but require many formulation-specific rate constants.<sup>1,3,17–19</sup>

Productive dose is converted to degree of cure through three operations:

**Local polymerisation rate:** \(B(I)=K_B I^{n_B}\).

**Effective cure time:** productive dose divided by local irradiance.

**Degree of cure:** \(1-\exp[-B(I)\times\text{effective cure time}]\).

The empirical rate \(B(I)\) collapses photoinitiator absorption, radical generation, propagation and termination into two fitted parameters, \(K_B\) and \(n_B\). The fitted exponent is required because radical photopolymerisation is generally sublinear in irradiance.<sup>8,15,16</sup> Consequently, equal optical dose delivered at different irradiances does not produce equal conversion. The initial capacities \(H_{\mathrm{O_2},0}\) and \(H_{\mathrm{T},0}\) similarly collapse inhibitor concentration, trapping stoichiometry and initiation efficiency into dose thresholds. For a fixed formulation, these capacities are estimated from \(H_0\approx I\,C(I)\), where \(C\) is the fitted induction time. The remaining reduced parameters are the optical spread \(\sigma_{\mathrm{opt}}\), oxygen and TEMPO diffusivities \(D_{\mathrm{O_2}}\) and \(D_{\mathrm{T}}\), pixel size \(\Delta x\), and time step \(\Delta t\).

We identify the kinetic and spatial parameters in separate experiments. Uniform projected circles provide a central region in which edge transport and optical spread are negligible. Each AIE or Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) trace is fitted to \(S(t)=A\{1-\exp[-B\max(t-C,0)]\}\), where \(A\) is the plateau, \(B\) is the post-induction rate and \(C\) is the induction time.<sup>5</sup> In the current DLP fluorescence dataset, 91 of 96 replicate traces passed the predefined fit-quality threshold. Across 0–5 mM TEMPO, the fitted rate followed \(B\propto I^{0.598-0.635}\) with \(R^2=0.943-0.999\). Synchronous FTIR measurements gave conversion-rate exponents of 0.473, 0.535 and 0.564 for 0, 1 and 3 mM TEMPO, respectively. The corresponding FTIR induction-time exponents were -1.100, -1.065 and -1.065, consistent with an approximately irradiance-independent inhibition capacity \(I\,C(I)\) over the tested range. By contrast, fluorescence-derived induction exponents ranged from -0.636 to -0.937. This difference shows that fluorescence onset is an observable-specific delay and should be transferred to chemical conversion using the paired FTIR–AIE calibration rather than inserted directly as an inhibitor concentration.

After fixing the kinetic parameters, patterned exposures identify the optical and transport terms. A concave corner is primarily sensitive to optical spill-in, whereas a convex boundary is sensitive to both optical spreading and replenishment of inhibitor from the surrounding dark resin. L-shaped and hollow patterns therefore provide complementary constraints on \(\sigma_{\mathrm{opt}}\), \(D_{\mathrm{O_2}}\) and \(D_{\mathrm{T}}\). These parameters should be fitted against the full time-resolved AIE field and then tested on held-out geometries, rather than selected from a single final image. Increasing \(\sigma_{\mathrm{opt}}\) rounds concave corners and expands illuminated boundaries; increasing inhibitor capacity delays cure; increasing diffusivity prolongs inhibition at exposed edges; increasing \(K_B\) accelerates post-induction conversion; and decreasing \(n_B\) increases the relative contribution of dim, optically contaminated pixels.

The simulator outputs the complete fields \(I\), \(H_{\mathrm{O_2}}\), \(H_{\mathrm{T}}\), \(D_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(\alpha\) at every time step. These outputs locate cure onset, undercure, overcure, boundary displacement and the times required to reach prescribed conversion levels. The reduced formulation is substantially cheaper to calibrate and differentiate than a full radical-reaction network, while retaining the optical, inhibition and transport processes that determine in-plane fidelity. Its scope is deliberately restricted to an isothermal two-dimensional layer with a fixed point-spread function and constant effective diffusivities. It does not yet resolve photobleaching, viscosity-dependent transport, gelation, heat release, shrinkage, chain-length distributions or interlayer attenuation. During optimisation, the current code also uses a small capped pre-induction conversion surrogate to maintain non-zero gradients; this numerical term must be reported separately from the physical model and tested by ablation.

## B.2.2 Trajectory-constrained differentiable inverse projection

We term the optimisation method **trajectory-constrained differentiable inverse projection**. The term identifies all three defining features: the desired cure field is mapped back to a projector mask, gradients are propagated through the reaction–transport simulator, and the objective can constrain the cure trajectory at one or more specified times. This formulation places the method within model-based grayscale optimisation<sup>6,11,20,21</sup> and differentiable simulation.<sup>22</sup> It distinguishes the method from empirical mask correction and from a neural network trained on input–output image pairs.<sup>13</sup>

For a target geometry, the grayscale mask is treated as the design variable and constrained to the projector range. One forward simulation predicts a degree-of-cure image at each selected checkpoint. The optimiser then minimises:

**Total loss = Σ [checkpoint weight × spatial error between the predicted and requested DoC images] + mask-regularisation penalty.**

At each checkpoint, the spatial error is calculated from the pixel-by-pixel difference between the predicted DoC image and the requested DoC image. A checkpoint weight determines how strongly that time point affects the final mask. The optional regularisation penalty can discourage abrupt or experimentally unsupported grayscale variations. Automatic differentiation propagates the total loss through every optical convolution, inhibitor update and cure step. Adam then updates the mask, after which pixel values are clipped to the projector range. The present implementation additionally enforces a minimum value of 15/255 inside the target region. Because the forward parameters remain fixed, optimisation requires no training set and every mask correction remains connected to a stated physical or empirical term.<sup>22</sup>

The same objective supports two control strategies. With one checkpoint, endpoint-constrained inverse projection targets one degree of cure at one exposure time. This mode is appropriate when final geometry or a specified partial conversion is the sole requirement. With multiple checkpoints, trajectory-constrained inverse projection targets several conversion-time pairs with one static mask. The MSEC implementation used three checkpoints: no cure at an early time, the target shape at the beginning of the cure window, and the same target at the end of that window.<sup>24</sup> An early checkpoint therefore suppresses premature gelation, while the later checkpoints encourage all features to enter and remain within the requested cure window. Increasing the weight of an intermediate checkpoint prioritises temporal synchrony; increasing the terminal weight prioritises final boundary fidelity. Thus, a static projector image can be designed against a sampled cure trajectory even though the image itself does not change during exposure.<sup>10,23</sup>

The optimiser returns an experimentally projectable grayscale mask together with predicted inhibitor maps, conversion movies, checkpoint errors and cure-time maps. It can therefore answer both a design question, namely which mask should be projected, and a process question, namely when each feature reaches a prescribed conversion. This is useful for solidification windows in which fine and coarse features must gel together, for partial-cure states used in multimaterial bonding, and for identifying regions that cannot meet all checkpoint constraints under one static exposure. Such infeasibility is itself informative because it marks cases that require a dynamic projection sequence or a revised resin formulation.

The MSEC study provides a numerical proof of concept for the optimisation itself.<sup>24</sup> For a 200 × 200-pixel star, 100 optimisation epochs increased peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) from 15.66 to 22.91 dB when oxygen diffusion was the only distortion. When Gaussian optical spreading was the only distortion, PSNR increased from 16.98 to 18.12 dB. With both effects active, PSNR increased from 15.16 to 17.82 dB. One epoch required approximately 1-2 s on the reported laptop, giving a total optimisation time of about 2 min. Under the parameter values tested, optical spreading caused a larger fidelity loss than oxygen diffusion. These results demonstrate gradient propagation through the reduced reaction-transport model and recovery of a compensating grayscale mask. Because the study used theoretical parameter values and simulated DoC fields, it does not constitute experimental validation.

Subsequent experiments establish initial physical feasibility while defining the present boundary of the claim. At 30 mW cm\(^{-2}\) in TEMPO-free resin, endpoint optimisation generated projectable grayscale masks and improved the agreement of solid-pattern fluorescence contours with the target. Candidate checkpoint schedules extracted from the measured cure curves were 1.5 s and 3.5 s for the TEMPO-free formulation, and 5.5 s and 8.0 s for 5 mM TEMPO. The same parameterisation did not reproduce the experimental overcure of hollow features, and masks tested at 70 mW cm\(^{-2}\) did not improve fidelity consistently. These failures indicate missing geometry-dependent transport and incomplete transfer of kinetic parameters across irradiance. They prevent a general experimental-performance claim at this stage. A paper-level validation should compare binary projection, endpoint-only optimisation and multi-checkpoint optimisation on held-out solid and hollow geometries, and report intersection-over-union, boundary error, retained void area, time-to-40% DoC, time-to-90% DoC, and replicate variability.

## Author-only consistency checks before submission

- `blur_size=50e-6` in the current code defines Gaussian kernel support, not a 50 µm standard deviation. With \(\Delta x=7.6\) µm, the code generates a 7-pixel kernel with an OpenCV standard deviation of about 1.4 pixels, or 10.6 µm.
- The current simulator hard-codes \(B=0.1131I^{0.6038}\), whereas the present analysis pipeline returns \(B=0.1144I^{0.5977}\) for 0 mM TEMPO. Freeze the trial-rejection rules and regenerate the parameter table before reporting coefficients.
- The main optimisation function presently returns only the terminal loss. The early and intermediate terms are calculated but commented out. Multi-checkpoint optimisation must be enabled explicitly before it is described as the executed method.
- The code targets 0.77 conversion at the intermediate checkpoint and 0.90 at the terminal checkpoint, whereas the Q7 notes identify checkpoint times from 0.40 and 0.90 conversion. Select one definition and use it consistently in code, figures and text.
- The current comments label 34.0231, 67.9730 and 97.4406 mJ cm\(^{-2}\) inconsistently. Their intended association with 1, 3 and 5 mM TEMPO should be confirmed before publication.
- The capped pre-induction term, `min(0.002 × cumulative dose, 0.015)`, is an optimisation surrogate. It should not be interpreted as measured conversion without independent validation.

## B.2.1 AIE-calibrated reduced reaction-transport model

We developed an aggregation-induced emission (AIE)-calibrated reduced reaction-transport model to predict how a projected grayscale image evolves into a spatially non-uniform degree-of-cure (DoC) field. The model retains the four processes that most directly determine in-plane printing fidelity: redistribution of projected light, transport of dissolved inhibitors, consumption of oxygen and TEMPO, and polymerisation after local inhibition has been overcome. A single layer is discretised into a two-dimensional grid. Each pixel stores its local irradiance, remaining oxygen capacity, remaining TEMPO capacity, accumulated productive exposure and DoC. The present implementation uses a 7.6 µm pixel pitch and a 0.05 s time step. This state-based representation preserves the spatial and temporal coupling needed to reproduce rounded corners, contracted boundaries, light bleed and delayed curing without solving a full radical-reaction network at every pixel.

The simulation first converts the commanded grayscale mask into the irradiance actually delivered to the resin. The nominal irradiance is multiplied by the normalised grayscale value of each pixel, and the resulting image is convolved with a Gaussian point-spread function. This convolution represents projector defocus, diffraction, scattering and optical cross-talk between neighbouring pixels. It is evaluated once before the kinetic simulation because the current model assumes that the optical point-spread function does not change during conversion. Consequently, a bright pixel can increase irradiance in adjacent dark pixels, causing facilitative overcure, while its own peak irradiance can be reduced by spatial spreading. Point-spread and convolution models have been used previously to describe this optical proximity effect and to design compensating projection masks (refs. 4,6,11,20,21).

The chemical calculation then advances the inhibitor and cure fields at every time step. Oxygen and TEMPO are represented as energy-equivalent inhibition capacities, expressed as the exposure required to exhaust each inhibitor reservoir. Before reaction, the oxygen and TEMPO fields are transported laterally using Gaussian convolution kernels derived from their effective diffusivities. This operation approximates constant-diffusivity Fickian transport while avoiding the restrictive time-step condition of a fully explicit finite-difference solver. Reflective padding imposes an approximately no-flux boundary at the edge of the simulated domain. The current implementation uses provisional effective diffusivities of approximately 400 µm² s⁻¹ for oxygen and 300 µm² s⁻¹ for TEMPO; an additional fitted mobility factor narrows the TEMPO transport kernel. These quantities are process parameters rather than universal molecular constants because they also absorb the effects of formulation, viscosity, numerical resolution and the finite convolution kernel.

After transport, the local exposure delivered during the time step is allocated sequentially. Exposure first consumes the remaining oxygen capacity. Any exposure left after oxygen depletion consumes TEMPO, and only exposure remaining after both inhibitor capacities reach zero is accumulated as productive curing exposure. This ordering reflects the strong competition of oxygen and persistent radical scavengers with chain propagation, while avoiding the need to track photoinitiator, primary radicals, propagating radicals, peroxyl radicals and terminated chains separately. Full kinetic models can describe those species explicitly, but commonly require tens of formulation-specific rate constants and transport parameters (refs. 1,3,17-19). The sequential capacity model therefore acts as a deliberate coarse graining of the induction period rather than a claim that the underlying reactions occur as perfectly separated stages.

Productive exposure is converted into DoC using a delayed exponential response. The post-induction rate follows a fitted power law in the local irradiance. Its coefficient sets the overall curing speed, whereas its exponent describes the sensitivity to irradiance. Productive exposure divided by local irradiance defines the effective curing time, after which DoC approaches unity exponentially at the local rate. This power-law dependence is essential because the measured rate is sublinear in irradiance; equal doses delivered at different irradiances therefore do not produce equal conversion histories (refs. 8,15,16). The fitted coefficient and exponent jointly absorb photoinitiator absorption, radical generation, propagation, bimolecular termination and conversion-dependent mobility. The initial oxygen and TEMPO capacities similarly absorb inhibitor concentration, trapping stoichiometry and initiation efficiency.

The kinetic parameters are identified independently of optical spreading and lateral diffusion. Large, uniformly illuminated circles provide a central region sufficiently far from an edge that the local fluorescence and FTIR traces are dominated by reaction kinetics. Each trace is fitted to a delayed exponential with three quantities: a plateau A, a post-induction rate B and an induction time C. In the current DLP fluorescence dataset, 91 of 96 replicate traces passed the predefined fit-quality criterion. Across 0, 1, 3 and 5 mM TEMPO, the fitted fluorescence rate followed a power law with intensity exponents between 0.598 and 0.635 and coefficients of determination between 0.943 and 0.999. Increasing TEMPO reduced the fitted rate coefficient and increased the induction time, consistent with stronger radical inhibition.

Synchronous FTIR and fluorescence measurements provide the conversion calibration required to interpret the AIE signal. FTIR-derived DoC rates had intensity exponents of 0.473, 0.535 and 0.564 for 0, 1 and 3 mM TEMPO, respectively. Their induction-time exponents were -1.100, -1.065 and -1.065. Because these values are close to -1, the product of irradiance and induction time is approximately constant for a fixed formulation, supporting the use of an energy-equivalent inhibitor capacity. By comparison, fluorescence-derived induction-time exponents ranged from -0.636 to -0.937. Fluorescence onset therefore cannot be treated directly as chemical inhibitor depletion. Instead, paired FTIR-AIE measurements are used to map the fluorescence-derived kinetic parameters onto conversion-derived rate and induction parameters. This distinction allows AIE to provide spatially resolved operando measurements while FTIR supplies the chemical DoC scale (ref. 5).

After the kinetic parameters are fixed, patterned exposures identify optical spreading and inhibitor transport. Concave and convex corners provide complementary information. Optical spill-in affects both types of corner, whereas diffusion of inhibitor from dark resin predominantly suppresses cure near exposed outer boundaries. L-shaped, solid and hollow patterns can therefore be fitted jointly to separate the point-spread width from the oxygen and TEMPO transport parameters. Increasing optical spread rounds concave corners, fills narrow gaps and expands illuminated boundaries. Increasing inhibitor capacity delays cure throughout the exposed region. Increasing diffusivity preferentially delays exposed boundaries because inhibitor is replenished from neighbouring dark resin. Increasing K_B accelerates post-induction conversion, while a smaller n_B increases the relative importance of dim pixels receiving scattered light. The final parameters should be fitted against the complete time-resolved AIE field and validated on geometries excluded from calibration.

The model outputs the irradiance, oxygen capacity, TEMPO capacity, productive exposure and DoC fields throughout the exposure. These fields predict when and where polymerisation begins, how rapidly each feature approaches a prescribed DoC, and which regions become undercured or overcured. They also provide spatial maps of the times required to reach partial and final conversion. The model is sufficiently compact to run repeatedly during inverse design and sufficiently interpretable to associate a prediction error with optics, kinetics or transport. Its present scope is an isothermal two-dimensional layer with constant effective diffusivities and a conversion-independent point-spread function. It does not yet resolve heat release, photobleaching, gelation, shrinkage, conversion-dependent diffusion, chain-length distributions, axial attenuation or interactions between successively printed layers. These boundaries define the conditions under which its predictions should be interpreted.

## B.2.2 Differentiable inverse projection with endpoint and trajectory constraints

We term the overall method differentiable inverse projection. The single-checkpoint mode is endpoint-constrained inverse projection, whereas the multi-checkpoint mode is trajectory-constrained inverse projection. These terms describe the operation more precisely than “direct optimisation”: the method begins with a requested spatial DoC field and works backwards to the grayscale projector mask predicted to produce it. It is differentiable because gradients are propagated through the optical, transport and curing calculations. The method belongs to the broader class of model-based grayscale compensation and differentiable physical simulation (refs. 6,11,20-22), but it does not require a neural network or an experimentally generated training set.

The optimisation treats every grayscale pixel in the projected mask as an adjustable variable. During each optimisation epoch, the current mask is passed through the complete forward simulator. The simulator first generates the blurred irradiance field, then advances oxygen, TEMPO, productive exposure and DoC through the requested exposure period. The predicted DoC image is retrieved at each selected checkpoint and compared pixel by pixel with the requested DoC image. Automatic differentiation calculates how changing every projector pixel would change the total error. Adam uses these gradients to update the mask, after which the grayscale values are clipped to the available projector range. The current implementation also maintains a minimum grayscale of 15/255 inside the intended cure region so that target pixels do not depend entirely on scattered light.

The optimisation objective can be stated without additional notation: total loss equals the weighted spatial error at every selected checkpoint, plus an optional penalty on the grayscale mask. The spatial error measures the difference between the predicted and requested DoC images over all pixels. A checkpoint weight determines the relative importance of that time point. The optional mask penalty can suppress abrupt grayscale variations, isolated pixels or values that are difficult for the projector to reproduce. In the earlier MSEC implementation, the loss contained three time points: the target remained uncured at an early checkpoint, reached the desired shape at the beginning of the cure window and retained that shape at the end of the cure window (ref. 24). This creates a finite operating window during which the exposure can be stopped without losing the intended geometry.

The framework supports two distinct operating strategies. Endpoint-constrained inverse projection uses one requested DoC and one exposure time. It is appropriate when the final geometry or a single partial-cure state is the principal requirement. Trajectory-constrained inverse projection uses several checkpoints with one static grayscale mask. An early zero-cure checkpoint penalises premature polymerisation. An intermediate checkpoint can force fine and coarse features to approach the same partial conversion at the same time. A final checkpoint enforces the terminal DoC and geometry. Increasing the intermediate weight favours temporal synchronisation, whereas increasing the final weight favours terminal boundary fidelity. These alternatives explain the trade-off observed in the Q7 studies: adding an intermediate checkpoint improved synchronisation but could reduce final-shape accuracy, while final-only optimisation produced better terminal fidelity but allowed different features to cure at different times. The present production script is configured in endpoint mode and backpropagates only the terminal error; the multi-checkpoint objective is a previously evaluated extension that can be re-enabled after its checkpoint targets and weights are fixed.

The MSEC study provides the first numerical proof of concept for this optimisation architecture (ref. 24). For a 200 by 200-pixel star, 100 optimisation epochs increased peak signal-to-noise ratio from 15.66 to 22.91 dB when oxygen diffusion was the only distortion. When Gaussian optical spreading was the only distortion, optimisation increased the value from 16.98 to 18.12 dB. With both oxygen diffusion and optical spreading active, it increased from 15.16 to 17.82 dB. One epoch required approximately 1-2 s on the reported laptop, corresponding to a total optimisation time of about 2 min. Under the tested parameter values, optical spreading caused a larger loss of fidelity than oxygen diffusion. The optimised masks also developed physically interpretable features: low-intensity halos depleted oxygen near vulnerable corners, while local edge corrections compensated for optical spreading. Because these calculations used theoretical parameters and simulated DoC fields, they establish differentiability and numerical compensation rather than experimental accuracy.

Subsequent printing experiments provide initial physical evidence and expose the current model boundary. At 30 mW cm⁻² in the TEMPO-free formulation, the optimised grayscale masks improved the correspondence between solid-pattern fluorescence contours and the target. Candidate schedules derived from the measured cure curves placed partial and final checkpoints at 1.5 and 3.5 s for the TEMPO-free resin, and at 5.5 and 8.0 s for 5 mM TEMPO. The same parameter set did not reproduce the overcuring observed experimentally in hollow structures, indicating that transport across enclosed boundaries is not yet represented adequately. Masks tested at 70 mW cm⁻² also failed to improve fidelity consistently, showing that parameters calibrated at lower irradiance cannot yet be transferred across the complete operating range. These results support feasibility at one condition while preventing a general performance claim.

The immediate output of the optimisation is a projectable grayscale mask. The same calculation also produces predicted DoC movies, inhibitor maps, cure-onset maps, checkpoint errors and local times to partial or final conversion. These outputs allow the method to determine whether one static mask can satisfy a requested cure trajectory. If no mask can satisfy all checkpoints, the residual error identifies where the specification conflicts with the resin kinetics or optical resolution. That information can motivate a dynamic projection sequence, a different exposure intensity, modified checkpoint weights or a revised inhibitor concentration. A definitive validation should compare binary projection, endpoint-only optimisation and multi-checkpoint optimisation on held-out solid and hollow geometries. Performance should be reported using intersection-over-union, boundary displacement, retained void area, time-to-40% DoC, time-to-90% DoC and variability across independent replicates.
